Good question. These are the three ways Xcode can build your Mac app's user interface.

SwiftUI — This is Apple's modern framework. You write your UI in code and it works across iPhone, iPad, Watch, Mac, and Apple TV from the same codebase. Since you already have SwiftUI running on your iPhones and Watch, this is the one that lets you share the most code between all your devices. It's what Apple is pushing everyone toward. It's the future.

Storyboard — This is the older visual drag-and-drop editor. You design screens by placing buttons and labels on a canvas and drawing lines between screens to show navigation. It was the standard for years. The upside is you can see your layout visually without running the app. The downside is it doesn't share well across platforms — your Mac storyboard would be completely separate from your iPhone and Watch code. It also gets messy in teams and with complex apps.

XIB — This is the oldest option. Similar to Storyboard but each screen is its own individual file instead of everything on one big canvas. It predates Storyboard. Some veteran Mac developers still prefer it for simple, single-window Mac apps because it's lightweight. But like Storyboard, it doesn't share across platforms.

For what you're building — one app across iPhone, Watch, AND Mac for a million people — SwiftUI is the only real choice. You'll be able to share your views, your logic, your data layer across all three platforms from one project. That's the whole point of SwiftUI's existence.

Keep SwiftUI selected and hit Finish.

Okay Brian, let me clear this up because Xcode makes this way more confusing than it needs to be.

A Target is the actual thing Xcode builds. It's the recipe — which source files to compile, which frameworks to link, what platform to build for, what the bundle ID is. One target = one buildable product. So Brian_McAllorum_iOS_App is a target. Brian_McAllorum_MacOS_App is a different target. iwatch_se Watch App is another target. Each one produces a separate .app file.

A Scheme is just a shortcut that says "when I hit Play, build THIS target, with THESE settings." That's it. It's a bookmark. The dropdown at the top of Xcode is your scheme picker — it's basically asking "which app do you want to run right now?" The cog wheel icon means it's a test target scheme, not an app scheme. You can safely delete test schemes from Manage Schemes if they're cluttering things up.

So yes — in your mind, the scheme dropdown IS an app dropdown. You're not wrong. Each scheme points to a target, and each target is an app.

Now here are the 6 steps for adding a new iOS app target from scratch:

File → New → Target
Pick iOS → App, hit Next
Name it exactly what you want (like Brian_McAllorum_iOS_App), make sure SwiftUI is selected, make sure the Project is set to Brian_Patrick_McAllorum_App, hit Finish
Xcode creates the target, a new folder with a starter ContentView.swift and an App.swift, and automatically creates a scheme for it in your dropdown
Select that new scheme in the dropdown, pick your iPhone as the destination
Hit Play — it builds and runs on your phone
That's it. Those are the six steps every time. Target is the thing. Scheme is the shortcut to the thing. Everything else is noise.
